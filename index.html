Controlling tissue size by active fracture

when growth and rupture compete, mechanics becomes a size-control mechanism

Wei Wang (汪巍) · Brian A. Camley

Department of Physics & Astronomy · Department of Biophysics · Johns Hopkins University

📄 Phys. Rev. E 113, 034405 (2026) 📐 arXiv:2503.03126 ⌥ Reproduction code

This paper builds a quantitative bridge from active cell mechanics to lineage-size statistics: motility and junction rupture set an effective break rate $k_b$, and the ratio $k_b / k_d$ determines how large or variable a tissue-like cluster can become.

Abstract

Overview

TL;DR

The paper shows that cluster size can be controlled by a balance between cell division and motility-driven fracture. It derives rupture rates from active-particle mechanics, exact lineage-size distributions from stochastic growth models, and tests the picture in 2D simulations.

Groups of cells can both grow and break apart. This paper asks what physical factors determine those fractures, and what sets the eventual size distribution of a growing cluster. The starting point is a one-dimensional chain of active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles connected by springs.

From that microscopic model, the paper derives how break rate depends on active speed, persistence time, and junction mechanics. It then coarse-grains the problem into a lineage model where growth and fracture compete. In steady state, the key control parameter becomes $$\gamma = \frac{k_b}{k_d},$$ the ratio of fracture rate to division rate.

The resulting theory predicts not just mean size, but full cluster-size distributions, their variability, and a striking survival law for intact clusters in the all-cells-divide case. Finally, a 2D active-cluster simulation tests which pieces of the 1D theory remain accurate in a more realistic geometry.

Motivation

From tissue rupture to a theory of size control

The paper is motivated by systems where fracture is not an accident but a recurring part of the biology: germline cysts, multicellular clusters, and simple organisms that grow and split. The question is whether random active fracture can itself regulate size.

⛓

Mechanics

Active cells stretch the bonds that hold a cluster together. Those bond fluctuations are collective, not just pairwise, so the rupture problem is already nontrivial at the mechanical level.

⌁

Statistics

Once rupture is converted into a rate $k_b$, the cluster becomes a stochastic birth-fracture process. Growth rules then determine whether sizes remain broad or become tightly controlled.

◧

Validation

The final step is to test the 1D picture in a 2D model where cells divide, bonds break and reform, and daughter clusters are selected after rupture events.

The central claim is compact: if mechanics sets the break rate $k_b$ and division sets the growth rate $k_d$, then lineage-size statistics are largely organized by $\gamma = k_b / k_d$. Restricting where growth happens, or where fracture can occur, can dramatically improve size control.

The Model

A chain model, a rupture law, and exact growth-fracture statistics

The theoretical structure of the paper has three layers: a mechanical active-particle model, a coarse-grained birth-fracture lineage process, and a survival calculation at steady state.

Three conceptual layers

1

1D active chain. Cells are active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles linked by springs, with motion determined by active noise, persistence time $\tau$, mobility $\mu$, and stiffness $k$.

2

Rupture rate. A stretched link escapes over a threshold barrier, yielding a Kramers-style break rate $k_b$ that depends on speed, persistence, and junction properties.

3

Growth-fracture lineage. Cells divide at rate $k_d$ while rupture splits the cluster and one daughter lineage is retained, giving exact stationary distributions for several growth rules.

4

Steady-state survival. In the all-cells-divide model, the intact-cluster survival probability collapses to a simple exponential controlled only by $k_d$.

Key relations

Control parameter
$$\gamma = \frac{k_b}{k_d}$$
All-cell growth steady state
$$p_n = \frac{\gamma}{(1+\gamma)^n}$$
Universal survival law for all-cell growth
$$S(t)=e^{-k_d t}$$

The paper is explicit that this survival result is conditional, not universal across every possible model. It relies on the all-cell growth mechanism and on a class of fragmentation kernels with the right dependence on pre-fracture cluster size.

Results

Results Across Scales

The results move from microscopic rupture mechanics to lineage-size statistics, then to the survival law for intact clusters, and finally to two-dimensional validation.

1D theory

The 1D chain yields a collective rupture rate, exact growth-fracture distributions, and the steady-state survival law.

2D validation

The 2D simulations do not directly use the 1D rupture formula. They measure an effective break rate and test whether the lineage statistics still collapse onto the 1D theory.

Survival of an intact cluster has a simple steady-state form. In the all-cell-growth model, the survival probability collapses to $S(t)=e^{-k_d t}$. Varying $k_b$ leaves the curve unchanged, while varying $k_d$ rescales the decay timescale.

Key Findings

Key Takeaways

01

Collective rupture is not pairwise rupture

A bond embedded in a long active chain has different fluctuation statistics from a two-particle link. That changes the effective escape problem and therefore the break rate.

02

$\gamma = k_b / k_d$ organizes the size statistics

Once rupture is coarse-grained into $k_b$, the steady-state lineage-size distributions are controlled by the competition between fracture and division.

03

Where growth and rupture happen matters

Restricting division to the ends, or localizing rupture toward the center, tightens size control compared with the fully homogeneous all-cell growth model.

04

The survival law is elegant but conditional

The exponential survival result $S(t)=e^{-k_d t}$ is exact for the all-cell growth model at steady state, but it is not meant to hold for every alternative growth or rupture rule.
